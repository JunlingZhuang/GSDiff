Nestwork: Conditional 3D Furnished House Layout Generation through Latent
Heterogeneous Graph Diffusion
Anonymous CVPR submission
Abstract
This paper introduces Nestwork, a unified latent-diffusion 001
framework for conditional 3D furnished house layout gen- 002
eration using a heterogeneous graph of rooms and furni- 003
ture. Designing reasonable and controllable 3D layouts 004
that reflect the underlying semantic structure of a house is 005
a key challenge in AI-assisted architectural design. Exist- 006
ing graph-based methods either produce unfurnished multi- 007
room layouts or generate furnished scenes one room at a 008
time, preventing joint reasoning over room structure and 009
furniture placement. Nestwork represents an entire house 010
as a heterogeneous graph with typed room and furniture 011
nodes and multiple spatial relations. A single unconditional 012
autoencoder based on a heterogeneous graph attention net- 013
work embeds this graph into a compact latent space, and 014
a low-rank relational field compensates for missing geo- 015
metric edge information at test time. A diffusion denoiser 016
is trained once using random masking, enabling the same 017
model to operate under different conditioning strengths, 018
from topology-only to fully annotated graphs. Multi-level 019
conditioning combines masked node-level attention with 020
graph-level embeddings to support flexible user control, in- 021
cluding layouts specified through natural-language descrip- 022
tions. Experiments on the 3D-FRONT dataset show that 023
Nestwork achieves high fidelity, structural consistency, and 024
diversity. Controlled ablations further validate the contri- 025
butions of each component.026
1. Introduction027
Generating fully furnished 3D house layouts is a founda- 028
tional task in architectural design, mixed reality, and vir- 029
tual environment synthesis. Professional applications re- 030
quire solutions that capture both theglobal structureof 031
room adjacencies, and thelocal precisionof furniture place- 032
ment and geometry. Scene graphs offer a natural and inter- 033
pretable interface for controlling layout generation: users 034
can insert, delete, or mask nodes and edges to constrain 035the structure while deferring geometric inference to the036
model. However, existing methods divide the task by037
spatial scale. House-level models generate unfurnished038
layouts using room graphs alone [14, 16, 26, 33], while039
room-level models generate furniture arrangements in iso-040
lation [1, 6, 48, 49]. Multi-stage systems that combine both041
suffer from error accumulation and spatial inconsistencies042
due to recursive and disjoint pipelines.043
Merging rooms and furniture into asinglegraph intro-044
duces two key challenges. First, the semantic and geomet-045
ric disparity between rooms and furniture requires feature-046
aware modeling: rooms encode high-level spatial logic such047
as adjacency and containment, while furniture carries fine-048
grained geometric attributes and diverse categories. Ho-049
mogeneous GNNs [32], which share parameters across all050
nodes, often fail to capture this structural heterogeneity051
and result in oversmoothing. In contrast, Heterogeneous052
GNNs (HGNNs) [40] preserve type-specific message pass-053
ing and are more appropriate for multi-scale scene synthe-054
sis. Second, the generator must support diverse condition-055
ing modes, from unlabeled topologies to fully annotated056
graphs without requiring retraining or hierarchical schedul-057
ing. While diffusion models [13] have shown strong poten-058
tial for improving fidelity in scene-graph-conditioned 3D059
synthesis [22, 48, 49], most existing denoising backbones060
are built on 1D U-Nets [31] or Transformers [37] treat061
nodes independently and lack explicit structural inductive062
bias. Although recent work [1, 49] incorporates graph-level063
message passing during denoising, it still relies on homoge-064
neous representations. In general, the use of graph-aware,065
type-sensitive diffusion backbones remains underexplored066
in 3D layout generation.067
Nestworkaddresses these limitations with a unified gen-068
erative framework that operates directly on aheterogeneous069
house graphcapturing rooms, furniture, and typed spa-070
tial relations. A single unconditional autoencoder com-071
presses the entire house graph into a latent representation,072
where a heterogeneous graph attention backbone preserves073
type-specific structure and a low-rank relational field pro-074
vides relational context without requiring geometric edge075

2. Related works103
2.1. Graph-constrained Layout Generation104
Scene graphs have long served as structured representations 105
for image retrieval and manipulation [18, 24, 43] and now 106
underpin architectural layout generation across house and 107
room scales. At thehouselevel, models such as House- 108
GAN [26], Graph2Plan [14], HouseDiffusion [33], and GS- 109
Diff [16] map room-relation or structural graphs to 2D floor 110plans under geometric constraints (e.g., wall alignment,111
boundary adherence) but omit explicit 3D furniture layouts.112
At theroomlevel, methods capture fine-grained object rela-113
tions via semantic or hierarchical graphs: GRAINS [20] and114
SceneHGN [10] use recursive graph V AEs, while Graph-115
to-3D [6] trains a conditional graph V AE to complete116
3D arrangements from user-specified graphs. Recent ap-117
proaches [1, 5, 22, 29, 39, 48, 49] introduce autoregressive118
or diffusion priors to boost fidelity.119
Furnished house-scale generation has recently emerged:120
CHOrD [35] generates collision-free 3D layouts from text121
or floor-plan inputs using diffusion and retrieval, but re-122
mains globally conditioned without explicit room–furniture123
graphs or masked-graph control. LLM-based agents [7, 9,124
46] also synthesize layouts from text via reasoning and re-125
trieval, using graphs only as transient reasoning aids rather126
than learned conditioning. To our knowledge, no prior work127
unifies rooms and furniture within a heterogeneous graph to128
enablesingle-pass3D generation with flexible, multi-level129
conditioning as proposed in our framework.130
2.2. Heterogeneous graphs131
Heterogeneous graphs (HGs) offer a principled framework132
for representing complex relational structures with multiple133
node and edge types. They have been widely adopted in134
domains such as bibliographic networks, where they dif-135
ferentiate between relations likeAuthor–Publishes–Paper136
andPaper–Cites–Paper. In graph mining, Heterogeneous137
Graph Neural Networks (HGNNs) exploit this typed struc-138
ture using mechanisms such as meta-paths and type-aware139
attention to capture semantic and topological signals for140
tasks including node classification and link prediction [17,141
27, 40]. In computer vision, HGs have been used for 2D142
scene graph generation to model distinct entity categories143
such as humans, animals, and objects, as demonstrated in144
HetSGG [47]. These approaches demonstrate the utility of145
HGs for fine-grained relational reasoning across semanti-146
cally diverse elements.147
2.3. Diffusion Models148
Diffusion models [13] synthesize data by reversing a noise149
process, while latent diffusion [31] improves efficiency by150
denoising in a compressed feature space. For 3D scenes,151
non-graph methods diffuse (i) over voxelized or gridded152
fields—denoising feature or occupancy grids with U-Net153
backbones [3, 19, 30, 45]—or (ii) over unordered object154
sets by denoising per-object attributes such as size, posi-155
tion, and category [15, 36]. To capture structural priors,156
graph-conditioned diffusion models have emerged: Com-157
monScenes [48], EchoScene [49], InstructScene [22] and158
FreeScene [1] integrate graph encoders with node-wise or159
graph-conditioned denoising to jointly model layout and160
shape. However, these frameworks rely on homogeneous161

3. Methods171
Nestwork performs diffusion directly in the latent space of 172
a heterogeneous house graph (§3.1), enabling joint reason- 173
ing over room adjacencies and furniture placement in a sin- 174
gle pass. At the core of our representation is a Heteroge- 175
neous Graph Attention Network (HetGAT) (§3.2), which 176
performs type-specific message passing over the three node 177
types and five relation types present in house-scale scenes. 178
A single unified autoencoder (§3.3) uses this HetGAT back- 179
bone to encode each node into a compact latent. Its un- 180
conditional decoder incorporates a low-rank relational field 181
(LRF) to reconstruct edge embeddings from node latents, 182
compensating for the absence of geometric edge features 183
at test time. On this latent graph, we train one HetGAT- 184
based diffusion denoiser (§3.4) under a 50% random mask- 185
ing scheme, allowing a single model to operate under varied 186
conditioning inputs. The denoiser supports multi-level con- 187
ditioning: (i) node-level masked semantic conditioning via 188
cross-attention, and (ii) graph-level conditioning through 189
the house bounding box and optional text embeddings 190
(§3.5). Optional post-processing aligns room boundaries 191
and inserts architectural details for visualization (§3.6).192

4. Experiments411
To our knowledge, no prior method can generate a fully 412
furnished 3D house from a single heterogeneous scene 413
graph. Existing approaches either generate room layouts 414
without furniture [14, 26, 33], place furniture at room scale 415
[5, 6, 20, 22, 48, 49], or synthesize houses without graph 416
conditioning [3, 10, 35]. In the absence of a directly 417
comparable baseline, we design a comprehensive evalu- 418
ation suite that examines each major component of our 419
framework under controlled conditions. Specifically, we 420
(i) benchmark against the strongest feasible graph-based 421
house-scale alternative—a two-stage HouseDiffusion + Dif- 422
fuScene pipeline, (ii) compare heterogeneous versus homo- 423
geneous GNN backbones in the autoencoder, (iii) evaluate 424
alternative latent priors (IID, autoregressive, diffusion), (iv) 425
test a single model under four conditioning schemes, and 426
(v) ablate key modules, including cross-attention and the 427
Low-Rank Relational Field. All studies use identical data 428
splits, metrics, and training setups, ensuring a fair and rig- 429
orous assessment of each design choice.430
4.1. Dataset431
Experiments are conducted on the 3D-FRONT dataset [8], 432
which provides 6,813 professionally designed furnished 433
houses. Building upon it, we construct a new heteroge- 434
neous house-graph dataset,HG-FRONT, that represents 435
each house as a multi-type graph of rooms, furniture, and 436
structural relations. Following preprocessing steps detailed 437
in §3.1, we adopt room-level filtering criteria from prior 438
work [22, 29, 36] and introduce additional house-level con- 439
straints to remove implausible layouts, such as invalid adja- 440
cencies. The resulting HG-FRONT dataset contains 3,110 441
high-quality house graphs and serves as the foundation for 442
all subsequent training and evaluation. Implementation de- 443
tails are provided in the appendix.444
4.2. Evaluation Metrics445
We evaluate generation quality at the house, room, 446
and furniture levels using five complementary metrics. 447
(1)FID/KID.Following [29, 33, 45, 48, 49], we report 448
Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) [12] and Kernel Incep- 449
tion Distance (KID) [2], computed from color-coded ren- 450
derings in which each object is visualized by a unique 451
class color [6, 36, 48]. Metrics are computed over eight 452uniformly sampled views (256×256 resolution) per gen-453
erated layout. (2)KL divergence.This metric quanti-454
fies how well the predicted label distribution of masked455
nodes matches the ground-truth label distribution across456
the dataset. (3)Room-graph constraint satisfaction.For457
each generated house, we verify whether room pairs labeled458
asadjacentorfarsatisfy their corresponding spatial con-459
straints, following [6, 33, 48, 49]. We report the percentage460
of correctly satisfied edges. (4)Furniture collision rate.461
Extending [23, 45], we compute pairwise 3D Intersection-462
over-Union (IoU) [52] between all furniture objects and re-463
port the proportion of pairs with non-zero overlap. (5)Lay-464
out diversity.Following [6, 48], we measure the standard465
deviation of 3D box sizes, positions (in meters), and yaw466
angles (in radians) over 10 random samples per input to as-467
sess generative diversity.468
4.3. Implementation Details469
All models are trained using a 70/15/15 train/validation/test470
split. Autoencoders use a latent dimension of 128. Opti-471
mization uses AdamW with weight decay 1e-4, an initial472
learning rate of 1e-4, and ReduceLROnPlateau scheduling473
(factor 0.5, patience 100 epochs, min LR 1e-6). Autoen-474
coders are trained for 400 epochs, and diffusion models475
for 2000 epochs with 200 denoising time steps on a single476
NVIDIA A100 (40GB) on Colab. All experiments are con-477
ducted in PyTorch with PyTorch Geometric; further details478
are available in the appendix material.479

5. Conclusion597
We have introducedNestwork, the first single-pass dif-598
fusion framework that jointly generates both room lay-599
outs and furniture arrangements from a unified hetero-600
geneous graph. Our heterogeneous GNN backbone and601
latent-graph diffusion model achieve strong realism, struc-602
tural compliance, and diversity on 3D-FRONT, while sup-603
porting flexible, mask-based conditioning.Despite these604
strengths, several limitations remain. The current sys-605
tem does not explicitly verify the functional plausibility606
of generated layouts—circulation paths, for example, may607
be obstructed. These constraints limit its use to ideation608
rather than finalized design, suggesting future work should609
incorporate algorithmic checks or planning-aware objec-610
tives. Additionally, Nestwork currently relies on struc-611
tured semantic graphs; extending Nestwork to accept multi-612
modal conditioning inputs —such as floor-plan images and613
sketches—could further enhance its usability in architec-614
tural design and interactive media.615
